The practice operates across 250+ professional valuation services across India — and within its artwork appraisal specialism, the defining characteristic is the integration of three competencies that most Indian appraisal practices hold only in part: object-level connoisseurship (the ability to examine a painting, sculpture, or cultural object physically and form a defensible expert opinion on what it is and what condition it is in), market intelligence (current and historical auction and gallery data across Indian and international venues), and statutory qualification (the Government Approved Valuer registration that makes the appraisal report acceptable to the Income Tax Department, the NCLT, the High Court, and every other Indian authority that reviews art appraisals).

Appraisal standards the practice applies

Every artwork appraisal engagement at A2Z Valuers is conducted under one or more of the following recognised appraisal standards, as the purpose and the audience require:

USPAP (Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice) — the internationally recognised standard for artwork appraisal, required by US institutions, international auction houses, and foreign courts and insurers. USPAP appraisals are required for charitable donation appraisals submitted to the IRS by US taxpayers donating Indian art.

IVSC (International Valuation Standards Council) — IVS 2022 — the international framework for valuation, used by the IBBI Valuation Standards for IBC purposes. A2Z Valuers’ IBC artwork appraisals are prepared in alignment with IVS methodology as incorporated into the IBBI Valuation Standards.

RICS Red Book (RICS Valuation — Global Standards) — the UK and international standard, used by the practice for appraisals submitted to UK courts, insurers, and probate authorities where the client is an NRI with UK-based assets or interests.

Income Tax Act, 1961 — Section 34AB qualified valuation — the Indian statutory standard, under which the Government Approved Valuer certification governs the report format and the basis of value for Indian tax purposes.

Every appraisal report identifies, on its face, the standard under which it is prepared — so that the receiving authority, the opposing counsel, or the reviewing court knows exactly what methodology was applied and what its limitations are.

The three competencies in detail

Competency 1 — Object Connoisseurship

Object connoisseurship is the ability to stand in front of an artwork and form a defensible expert opinion on what it is. This includes attribution analysis — does the work's execution, style, palette, and signature match the documented oeuvre of the attributed artist? Condition assessment — what is the state of the paint layer, the canvas or support, the framing, and any restoration or conservation interventions?

Materials identification — are the materials consistent with the claimed period and medium? And provenance review — does the ownership history support the attribution and the claimed legal status? Nitesh Shrivastava conducts every physical examination personally, with photography under standard, raking, and where appropriate ultraviolet illumination.

Competency 2 — Market Intelligence

Market intelligence is the ability to place an artwork in its market context with precision. For Indian modern masters, this means tracking Saffronart, AstaGuru, and Pundole's results continuously, and drawing on Christie's and Sotheby's South Asian Art sales for the highest-value comparables. For Indian miniatures, it means tracking Christie's Islamic and Indian Art sales and Sotheby's South Asian Art sales.

For folk and tribal art, it means drawing on NGMA acquisition records, Crafts Council data, and specialist dealer pricing — sources that an untrained appraiser will not know to access. For decorative arts, it means tracking Bonhams’ Indian and Islamic decorative arts sales. The breadth and currency of the comparables database is as important as the analytical skill applied to it.

Competency 3 — Statutory Qualification

Statutory qualification is the Government Approved Valuer registration that transforms an appraisal opinion into a legally admissible document under Indian law. Without the Government Approved Valuer registration, an artwork appraisal in India — however expertly prepared — is not a valuation for Income Tax Act purposes, not admissible as a Regulation 27 report under the IBC, and not accepted by nationalised banks as a collateral appraisal. Nitesh Shrivastava holds the full suite of Indian statutory qualifications, including the forensic valuation appointments under Sections 132(2) and 132(9D) that allow him to appraise artworks discovered in IT search-and-seizure proceedings — the most demanding appraisal context in the Indian market.
