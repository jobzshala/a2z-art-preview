Artwork Appraiser vs Artwork Valuer — Why the Distinction Matters and When You Need Each

In the Indian art market, the terms “artwork appraiser” and “artwork valuer” are used interchangeably by most clients — and by many practitioners. They should not be. Understanding the distinction helps you ask for the right professional for the right purpose, and helps you evaluate whether the document you receive will serve the purpose you need it for.

What an artwork valuer does

An artwork valuer issues a valuation certificate — a formally prescribed document under a specific Indian statute. The Income Tax Department requires a valuation under Section 34AB of the IT Act for capital-gain computations and forensic assessments. The NCLT requires a Regulation 27 valuation under the IBC CIRP Regulations. The ASI requires a valuation for Antiquities Act compulsory-acquisition compensation. The statutory valuation certificate is a document addressed to an authority — it must comply with the format, methodology, and disclosure requirements of that authority. A statutory valuation can be issued only by a qualified, registered Government Approved Valuer.

What an artwork appraiser does

An artwork appraiser renders an expert appraisal opinion — a professionally documented, methodology-grounded assessment of what an artwork is and what it is worth for a specific purpose. The appraisal may or may not be a statutory document; it may be issued for a purpose (pre-acquisition due diligence, charitable donation, estate planning) that does not require a statutory certificate. The appraisal is a more comprehensive document than a valuation certificate because it typically includes:

A detailed physical description of the artwork — artist, title, date, medium, dimensions, signature, inscription, and any identifying marks.

A condition report — an assessment of the work's physical state at the date of appraisal, including any losses, repairs, restoration, yellowed varnish, or other condition issues.

A provenance record — a documented ownership history from the artist or original source to the present owner, as far as the record allows.

An attribution analysis — where the attribution is not beyond doubt, the appraiser's reasoned opinion on the attribution question.

A methodology statement — a description of the approaches used (sales comparison, replacement cost, income approach) and the data sources consulted.

Comparable sales — a selection of auction and gallery sales of comparable works, with source citations.

A concluded value — the appraiser's opinion of value, expressed as a single figure or a range, with the basis of value stated (fair market value, replacement cost, liquidation value).

Appraiser certification — the appraiser's signed statement of qualification, independence, and compliance with the applicable appraisal standard.

When you need an appraiser vs when you need a valuer

Use this table as a guide — consult the practice for any engagement where the line is unclear:

Pre-acquisition due diligence — you need an appraiser. A statutory valuation certificate is not required; an independent expert opinion on the artwork's identity, condition, and fair market value is.

Income Tax capital gain — you need a valuer (specifically, a Government Approved Valuer under Section 34AB). The appraiser and the valuer are the same person at A2Z Valuers; the report format is the statutory one.

Art insurance (new policy or renewal) — you need an appraiser who can also issue a replacement-cost valuation certificate. The insurer requires a certificate; the certificate must be prepared by an expert who has physically examined the work.

Charitable donation (Indian tax position) — you need a valuer for the Indian Income Tax Act position. If the donee is a US institution or the donor has a US tax obligation, you additionally need a USPAP-standard appraisal.

Estate division or probate — you need an appraiser for the object-level examination and equitable-distribution analysis; the court will typically also require a formal valuation certificate.

IBC / NCLT Regulation 27 — you need a registered valuer compliant with the IBBI Valuation Standards. The appraisal must follow the IBC format; the registered valuer status is mandatory.

Pre-sale or auction — you need an appraiser for an independent second opinion on the auction house's estimate. A statutory valuation certificate is not required.

Litigation or forensic — you need an appraiser who is also a Government Approved Valuer — to combine expert appraisal opinion with the statutory authority the court requires.
