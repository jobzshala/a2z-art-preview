Art as a Financial Asset Class — The Case for Treating Art Seriously on the Balance Sheet

The Indian art market as an asset class

The global art market is estimated at approximately $65 billion annually, placing it alongside commodities and infrastructure as a significant alternative asset class. The Indian segment — dominated by Indian modern masters (Husain, Souza, Raza, Gaitonde), the contemporary Indian art market (Subodh Gupta, Atul Dodiya, Jitish Kallat), and the antiquities market — has generated benchmark auction results that confirm its asset-class credentials: M.F. Husain's Untitled (Gram Yatra) sold at Christie's for ₹119 crore on 19 March 2025, the highest price ever paid for an Indian artwork. A2Z Valuers appraised a private collection of Mughal jewellery and colonial Indian art valued at over ₹50 crore in early 2025, followed by a record sale — a transaction scale that confirms art's position as a serious institutional asset class in India.

Why Indian collectors and corporates are treating art as an asset

Several converging forces are driving the institutionalisation of art as a financial asset in India:

Portfolio diversification — art has historically low correlation with equity and debt markets, making it an effective portfolio diversifier for family offices and HNI portfolios in periods of equity volatility.

Store of value — works by established Indian modern masters have appreciated significantly over multi-decade holding periods, outperforming inflation and in many cases outperforming benchmark equity indices on a risk-adjusted basis.

Art-backed lending — Indian private banks and NBFCs have been developing art-lending products, accepting blue-chip art as collateral for secured credit facilities. The art asset must be independently appraised before any lending decision — and the appraiser must be both a Government Approved Valuer and a specialist in the art market.

Tax efficiency — long-term capital gains on art held for more than 24 months are taxed at 12.5% without indexation (post-23 July 2024) or 20% with indexation — rates that compare favourably with other asset classes in the right holding-period scenario.

Corporate art programmes — listed Indian companies and MNCs operating in India are increasingly maintaining corporate art collections as part of workplace culture, CSR, and brand positioning — with the collections requiring formal appraisal for insurance and balance-sheet purposes.

IBC and financial distress — as India's insolvency and bankruptcy framework matures, art assets appear with increasing frequency in the estates of corporate debtors — requiring Regulation 27 appraisals that Resolution Professionals must commission.

The appraisal discipline this requires

An art asset appraiser operating at this level must simultaneously understand fine-art market dynamics (auction results, gallery pricing, artist market development), financial asset methodology (liquidity analysis, loan-to-value, portfolio attribution), Indian accounting standards (Ind AS 16, Ind AS 38), Indian statutory frameworks (Income Tax Act capital gain, IBC Regulation 27, Antiquities Act 1972), and forensic appraisal discipline (for enforcement and litigation contexts).
