Registered Artwork Valuer vs Unregistered Opinion — Why the Distinction Is Decisive

The Indian artwork valuation market includes many participants who issue documents called “valuation reports”, “valuation certificates”, or “market opinions” — gallery owners, art dealers, auction house specialists, market commentators, and independent “experts” of various descriptions. None of these is a registered Government Approved Artwork Valuer under Indian law unless they hold a current Section 34AB registration and, for IBC purposes, IBBI registration. The practical consequences of using an unregistered opinion in a statutory context are significant and frequently expensive.

The comparison — twelve decisive differences

Statutory authority: A registered valuer’s report carries a statutory presumption under Section 34AB that the authority must engage with. An unregistered opinion has no statutory standing.

Income Tax Department acceptance: The IT Department is required to consider a registered valuer’s report. An unregistered opinion can be, and frequently is, simply disregarded.

NCLT acceptance: Regulation 27 CIRP artwork valuations must be from IBBI-registered valuers. An unregistered report is not compliant and will be challenged.

Bank acceptance: A bank empanels registered valuers specifically to ensure that the valuations it relies on for credit decisions meet its governance standards. An unregistered valuation does not meet those standards.

Court standing: A registered Government Approved Valuer is recognised by Indian courts as an expert witness in artwork valuation matters. An unregistered opinion-giver is not automatically accorded the same standing.

Independence: A registered valuer is required by their registration conditions to be independent of the parties to the transaction. A dealer or gallery issuing a “valuation” of works they sell has a commercial interest in the figure and is not independent.

Personal liability: A registered valuer is personally accountable for the accuracy of their reports, with deregistration, penalty, and prosecution as consequences for incorrect reports. An unregistered opinion-giver bears no comparable legal consequence.

Methodology disclosure: A registered valuer is required to disclose their methodology and comparables. An unregistered opinion-giver is not.

Credential verifiability: A registered valuer’s registration can be verified against the Government’s registered-valuer list. An unregistered opinion-giver’s self-described credentials cannot be verified.

Renewal and conduct scrutiny: A registered valuer’s registration is subject to periodic renewal and conduct review. An unregistered opinion-giver faces no external scrutiny of their conduct.

Insurance acceptance: A registered valuer’s replacement-cost certificate is accepted by art insurers as the basis for the sum insured. An unregistered dealer’s letter is not.

Forensic appointment: Only a registered valuer can be appointed by the Income Tax Department for search-and-seizure artwork valuations under Sections 132(2) and 132(9D). An unregistered opinion-giver cannot be so appointed.

What it costs to use the wrong valuer

The cost of commissioning an unregistered artwork opinion and then having it rejected by the relevant authority is not merely the fee paid for the rejected opinion. It includes: the cost of commissioning a new report from a registered valuer; the delay penalty and interest on any tax liability that has accrued in the interim; the cost of the additional professional advice required to explain the delay; and, in the worst case, the reassessment at the authority’s own estimate — which may be significantly higher than the figure a registered valuer would have established. The registered valuer fee is the insurance premium that prevents all of these costs.
