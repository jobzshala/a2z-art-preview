A Registered Artwork Valuer in India — What the Designation Means and Why It Matters

The legal definition

In India, a registered artwork valuer is a person who holds a current, valid registration under Section 34AB of the Income Tax Act, 1961 as a qualified valuer for artworks and cultural assets — and, for IBC and Companies Act purposes, is additionally registered on the IBBI Registered Valuers panel. Both registrations require the valuer to demonstrate: (a) prescribed educational qualifications; (b) a defined period of practical valuation experience in the relevant asset class; (c) successful completion of a valuation examination; and (d) ongoing compliance with conduct, renewal, and continuing education requirements. Registration is not self-declared. It is examined and approved by the relevant government authority — the Income Tax Department for Section 34AB, and the IBBI for the IBC panel. A person who calls themselves an “artwork valuer” without holding these registrations is not a registered artwork valuer under Indian law, and their reports do not carry the statutory authority that registered valuers’ reports carry.

What registration means for the report

The Income Tax Department cannot dismiss the report without engagement. Under Section 34AB, an Assessing Officer who disagrees with a registered valuer’s figure must provide reasoned grounds — they cannot simply substitute their own estimate without basis.

The NCLT expects a registered-valuer report for Regulation 27 CIRP artwork valuations. A Regulation 27 report from an unregistered valuer will be challenged by creditors and is unlikely to survive NCLT scrutiny.

Banks accept registered-valuer reports as collateral-valuation evidence. An empanelled registered valuer’s report satisfies the bank’s governance requirements; an unregistered valuer’s report does not.

Courts recognise the registered valuer as an expert witness. When an artwork valuation is contested in litigation, a registered Government Approved Valuer is the category of witness whose opinion the court is prepared to accept as expert evidence.

The valuer’s independence requirement

A registered artwork valuer is required by their registration conditions to be independent of the parties to the transaction. They cannot hold a financial interest in the outcome of the valuation; they cannot be employed by or commercially related to the party commissioning the report in a way that compromises their independence; and they must disclose any relationship that could give rise to a perception of conflict. This independence requirement is what makes the registered valuer’s report credible to the authorities that rely on it. A dealer who values their own stock is not independent. A gallery that values works it has just sold is not independent. A registered Government Approved Valuer with no commercial interest in the transaction is independent — and the registration carries that presumption.

The valuer’s personal liability

A registered artwork valuer is personally liable for the accuracy of the reports they issue. Under the Income Tax Act, a registered valuer who issues a report that is found to be incorrect — whether through negligence, recklessness, or deliberate misstatement — is subject to deregistration, penalty, and prosecution. Under IBC proceedings, a registered valuer who issues an incorrect CIRP valuation report faces scrutiny from the IBBI and from the NCLT. This personal liability is not incidental. It is the mechanism that makes the registration meaningful — because a valuer who signs a report bears the professional and legal consequences if that report is wrong. An unregistered opinion-giver bears no such consequences. Which is exactly why the registered valuer’s report is worth commissioning.
